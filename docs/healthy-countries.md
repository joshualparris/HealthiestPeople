# Lessons from countries with unusually strong health outcomes

_Last updated: 21 September 2026._

## Important framing

There is **no single scientifically accepted “healthiest country”**.

Countries can look excellent on life expectancy while doing poorly on smoking, alcohol, mental health, obesity, physical activity or health inequality. This project therefore uses **multiple indicators** and asks a narrower question:

> Which health behaviours and systems repeatedly appear in countries with strong longevity / avoidable-mortality outcomes, and which of those lessons are independently supported by peer-reviewed evidence?

The main comparison uses recent OECD / government data for Japan, Spain, Switzerland, Iceland, South Korea, Singapore and Australia.

## Japan — copy the food pattern, not every Japanese risk factor

### What stands out

OECD 2025 reports:
- life expectancy: **84.1 years**
- preventable mortality: **86 per 100,000**
- treatable mortality: **49 per 100,000**

OECD 2025 reports **no comparable self-reported-obesity value for Japan**. It does report that **51% of adults were classified as insufficiently active** and daily smoking remained **15.7%**. Other sources often describe Japan as having low obesity, but that claim should not be attributed to this OECD country note.

### Diet evidence

A 2022 systematic review/meta-analysis of prospective Japanese cohorts found that greater adherence to a Japanese-style dietary pattern was associated with lower cardiovascular, stroke and heart-disease mortality.

The useful components repeatedly include:
- vegetables
- fruit
- fish
- soy foods
- seaweed
- green tea
- dietary fibre
- plant protein

However, the same literature repeatedly identifies **high sodium** as a weakness.

Peer-reviewed sources:
- Japanese-style diet and cardiovascular mortality meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35631146/
- Ohsaki Japanese Diet Index review (mortality, disability, dementia): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35631172/
- Ohsaki survival analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30846323/
- Japan Public Health Center cohort: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32676701/
- Japanese diet + sodium caveat: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17317693/
- Sodium/potassium and CVD mortality: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18614741/
- Reduced-salt Japanese diet and 19-year mortality: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19021919/

### Transferable lesson

**Use the Japanese pattern as “more fish/soy/vegetables/fruit/whole foods, but keep salt sensible.”**

Do not infer that:
- every Japanese food is healthy;
- high-salt foods are harmless;
- everyone should eat seaweed or green tea;
- Japanese longevity is caused by diet alone.

## Spain — the strongest country-specific dietary trial

### What stands out

OECD 2025 reports:
- life expectancy: **84.0 years**
- preventable mortality: **92 per 100,000**
- treatable mortality: **50 per 100,000**
- self-reported obesity: **15%**
- insufficient activity: **25%**

But Spain also had:
- daily smoking **19.8%**
- alcohol consumption **11.1 L/person/year**

So the lesson is **not** “copy the Spanish lifestyle wholesale”.

### Mediterranean-diet evidence

Spain provides unusually strong experimental evidence because the PREDIMED randomised trial tested Mediterranean dietary patterns in adults at high cardiovascular risk.

The corrected/re-published trial found lower major cardiovascular-event incidence in Mediterranean-diet groups supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts versus the control diet.

Sources:
- Corrected PREDIMED trial: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897866/
- Review of PREDIMED experimental outcomes: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31817731/
- Cardiovascular-health review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30817261/
- OECD Spain: https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_15a55280-en/spain_d37fc9b6-en.html

### Transferable lesson

Build meals around:
- vegetables
- legumes
- whole grains
- nuts
- extra-virgin olive oil
- fish / useful protein
- minimally processed foods

The project **does not adopt wine/alcohol as a health intervention**.

## Switzerland — prevention and treatment quality both matter

OECD 2025 reports:
- life expectancy **84.3 years**
- preventable mortality **80 per 100,000**
- treatable mortality **34 per 100,000**
- self-reported obesity **12%**
- insufficient physical activity **22%**

Treatable mortality is especially useful because it reminds us that longevity is not just diet/exercise. **Effective medical care matters.**

Caveat:
- daily smoking prevalence was **16.1%**, slightly above the OECD average.

Source:
- https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_15a55280-en/switzerland_7139bf0d-en.html

### Transferable lesson

**Use ordinary healthcare properly.**

That means:
- blood-pressure follow-up
- clinically appropriate lipids / glucose / iron testing
- vaccination
- age/risk-appropriate screening
- evidence-based medication when indicated
- seeing persistent abnormalities through rather than “biohacking around” them

The goal is not to imitate Swiss healthcare spending.

## Iceland — population risk factors can move mortality dramatically

A peer-reviewed analysis of Iceland from 1981–2006 found:
- coronary-heart-disease mortality fell about **80%**
- the model attributed about **73%** of that decline to population risk-factor reductions
- major contributors included lower cholesterol, less smoking and lower systolic blood pressure
- treatments explained about **25%**

Source:
- Iceland CHD mortality analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21103050/

OECD's 2025 Iceland profile also notes very low smoking but increasing obesity.

Source:
- https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/country-health-profile-2025-country-notes_7e72146d/iceland_405be6b9/1f7c97a2-en.pdf

### Transferable lesson

The boring risk factors are not boring in effect:

- don't smoke
- know blood pressure
- improve lipids when indicated
- stay active
- use evidence-based treatment when needed

## Singapore — health is multi-factor, and prevention is organised

Singapore Ministry of Health reports resident life expectancy of **83.5 years in 2024**.

Its Healthier SG model explicitly focuses on:
- a regular family doctor
- personalised health planning
- screening
- vaccination
- lifestyle support
- chronic-disease prevention

Official sources:
- Singapore vital statistics: https://www.moh.gov.sg/others/resources-and-statistics/population-and-vital-statistics/
- Healthier SG / healthy living: https://www.moh.gov.sg/staying-healthy/healthy-living/

### Cohort evidence

In the Singapore Chinese Health Study, adherence to five healthier lifestyle factors was associated with substantially lower mortality and with estimated life-expectancy gains at age 50.

Factors were:
- healthier diet
- not smoking
- physical activity
- healthier BMI
- light-to-moderate alcohol in the study's historical score

This project **does not interpret the alcohol component as a recommendation to drink**, because current public-health guidance does not require alcohol for health.

Source:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31750895/

A 2025 Singapore Chinese Health Study analysis also associated higher ultra-processed-food intake with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality.

Source:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41024211/

### Transferable lesson

**Stack several ordinary healthy behaviours; don't search for one magic marker.**

## South Korea — an important warning against single-metric thinking

OECD 2025 reports:
- life expectancy **83.5 years**
- treatable mortality **45 per 100,000**
- self-reported obesity **5%**

But also:
- insufficient activity **61%**
- suicide **23 per 100,000**
- daily smoking **15.3%**

Source:
- https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_15a55280-en/korea_40b1d2b4-en.html

Korean sodium research also shows that national dietary traditions can have trade-offs; high sodium intake has been studied as a cardiovascular burden.

Source:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33029290/

### Transferable lesson

**Low obesity does not equal complete health.**

Never use body weight/BMI alone as the main score.

## Australia — a strong baseline with clear weaknesses

OECD 2025 reports:
- life expectancy **83.0 years**
- preventable mortality **99 per 100,000**
- treatable mortality **47 per 100,000**
- daily smoking only **8.5%**
- insufficient activity **28%**

But:
- self-reported obesity **25%**
- alcohol consumption **10.5 L/person/year**

Source:
- https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/health-at-a-glance-2025_15a55280-en/australia_7cad4f74-en.html

### Transferable lesson for this project

Joshua does **not** need to reject Australian health guidance and import an exotic foreign lifestyle.

Australia already performs well on longevity and healthcare outcomes.

The useful strategy is:
- keep Australia's strengths;
- borrow specific evidence-backed food/activity/prevention lessons from other countries;
- address personal gaps rather than national averages.

# What survives across the countries

## 1. Don't smoke

This is one of the clearest population-health lessons.

Countries such as Australia and Iceland have very low smoking rates, and Iceland's historical mortality analysis attributes a meaningful part of its coronary-mortality decline to smoking reduction.

## 2. Keep blood pressure and atherogenic risk under control

The Iceland data show how population blood-pressure/cholesterol changes can materially shift mortality.

For an individual this means:
- know your BP trend;
- follow lipids in ordinary care;
- use evidence-based treatment when clinically indicated.

## 3. Eat minimally processed, plant-rich meals with useful protein

The Japanese and Mediterranean patterns overlap more than they differ:
- vegetables
- legumes / soy
- fruit
- whole grains
- fish
- nuts/seeds
- mostly minimally processed foods

Evidence beyond one country:
- PREDIMED: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897866/
- Japanese diet meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35631146/
- UPF umbrella review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418082/
- Updated UPF umbrella review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38363072/

## 4. Move often; you do not need elite exercise

Large prospective evidence supports lower mortality risk with more physical activity.

Sources:
- >30 million-participant dose-response meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36854652/
- 2024 accelerometer cohort: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39389333/
- 2024 harmonised accelerometer meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39089430/
- Steps umbrella review/meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38901742/

## 5. Strength still matters

Country longevity statistics do not directly prove a strength-training effect, but independent cohort evidence supports muscle-strengthening activity as part of a healthy adult routine.

Source:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35228201/

## 6. Social health belongs in the model

Countries are not just food and healthcare systems.

Large prospective meta-analysis supports social connection as a health factor.

Source:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37337095/

## 7. Use healthcare, don't just optimise yourself

Low treatable mortality in Switzerland, Australia, Japan, Korea and Spain reinforces a simple point:

> healthy behaviour and competent healthcare are complements, not competitors.

# What this changes for Josh

Joshua already has:
- high everyday movement
- Monday Ultimate Frisbee
- Saturday parkrun / family activity
- recent sleep around 8 hours
- low smoking exposure (non-smoker)
- ordinary primary-care pathology
- a dairy-free Mediterranean-ish preference

So the country research **does not justify adding hours of exercise or expensive testing**.

## Exact practical changes

### Food

Keep the current dairy-free Mediterranean-ish plan, but make the Japan/Spain overlap explicit:

**Most days**
- vegetables
- fruit
- legumes / soy foods
- whole grains
- nuts/seeds
- fish or another useful protein
- extra-virgin olive oil where practical

**Do not add**
- high-salt Japanese foods because “Japan is healthy”
- wine because “Spain/Mediterranean”
- expensive imported longevity foods

### Salt

Because both Japanese and Korean evidence identify sodium as a cardiovascular issue:

- do not deliberately add extra salt for longevity;
- use ordinary Australian guidance / clinician advice;
- prioritise potassium-rich whole foods such as vegetables, legumes and fruit unless a clinician has given a reason not to.

### Exercise

No extra step target.

Keep:
- Monday Frisbee
- Tuesday + Thursday short strength
- Saturday parkrun / run-walk

This already captures:
- incidental movement
- vigorous/intermittent cardio
- aerobic work
- strength
- family/social connection

### Prevention

The Singapore/Swiss/Iceland lesson is to make ordinary preventive care boringly reliable:

- maintain a proper home-BP baseline
- repeat clinically appropriate lipids/glucose/iron
- vaccinations
- age/risk-appropriate screening
- investigate persistent abnormalities rather than self-treating around them

### Relationships

Frisbee, family movement, church/community and time with family are not distractions from health.

They fit the broader evidence that social isolation/loneliness are associated with higher mortality.

## How this evidence is used

A country observation is **not** promoted into Josh's plan merely because the country has long life expectancy.

It must also:
1. fit the person's context;
2. have independent evidence beyond the country;
3. have acceptable risk/cost/time;
4. not conflict with current Australian guidance or clinician advice.

# Project conclusion

The strongest country-level lesson is **convergence**.

Japan, Spain, Switzerland, Iceland, Singapore, Korea and Australia do not share one perfect diet or one perfect health system.

The repeatable signal is:

1. don't smoke
2. stay active
3. preserve strength
4. eat mostly minimally processed plant-rich food with useful protein
5. keep salt/alcohol sensible
6. avoid persistent obesity / metabolic deterioration
7. know BP/lipids/glucose risk
8. use preventive/evidence-based healthcare
9. maintain relationships/community
10. make all of it sustainable for decades

That is a much more defensible target than trying to live “like Japan”.

# Evidence behind the Josh Plan

_Last reviewed: 20 September 2026._

This page separates **official recommendations**, **peer-reviewed evidence** and **Josh-specific reasons**.

It is intentionally stricter than the public-biohacker profiles.

## 1. Two resistance-training sessions per week

### Josh-specific reason

Josh's recent movement volume is already high, while repeated Health Connect audits show relatively little structured resistance training. The missing training domain is therefore strength rather than more walking.

### Official guidance

Australia's 2026 adult 24-hour movement guidelines recommend:

- moderate-to-vigorous activity for 30 minutes or more on most days;
- **muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days/week**;
- mobility/balance/coordination activities on 3 or more days/week;
- several hours of light activity daily.

Official:
- https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years
- Evidence report: https://www.health.gov.au/resources/publications/australian-24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-adults-18-to-64-years-and-older-adults-65years

### Peer-reviewed evidence

1. **Currier et al., BJSM 2023 — systematic review/network meta-analysis, 178 strength studies.**  
   All evaluated resistance-training prescriptions improved strength versus no exercise; prescriptions with at least two sets or two sessions/week had a high probability of improving strength.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579494/

2. **Shailendra et al., American Journal of Preventive Medicine 2022 — systematic review/meta-analysis.**  
   Any resistance training was associated with lower all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality; dose-response results suggested substantial benefit at modest weekly volumes, while causality cannot be established from the observational mortality studies.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35599175/

3. **Momma et al., BJSM 2022 — systematic review/meta-analysis.**  
   Muscle-strengthening activity was associated with lower all-cause mortality and several major non-communicable diseases; combined strengthening + aerobic activity was associated with lower mortality than neither.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35228201/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9209691/

### Why only 20 minutes twice a week?

**40 min/week is a personal minimum-effective starting dose, not an official optimum.**

It is chosen because:
- it meets the *frequency* component of Australian guidance;
- it is realistic with work and children;
- Josh currently needs adherence more than maximal training volume;
- meta-analytic evidence shows even low/modest resistance-training volumes can produce benefit.

The dose can be progressed later if adherence and symptom response are good.

## 2. Keep Monday Ultimate Frisbee

### Josh-specific reason

Frisbee is one of Josh's most valuable existing habits because it combines:
- moderate/vigorous intermittent movement;
- enjoyment;
- social connection;
- a fixed weekly routine.

Documented sessions have ranged from moderate-to-vigorous to genuinely high load. It should therefore **replace**, not sit on top of, another hard interval session that day.

### Official guidance

Frisbee contributes to the Australian recommendation for regular moderate-to-vigorous activity.

- https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years

### Peer-reviewed evidence

There is limited direct adult Ultimate-Frisbee outcome research, so the evidence here uses the closest defensible proxy: **adult team/recreational sport**.

1. **Eather et al., Systematic Reviews 2023 — systematic review, 29 adult studies.**  
   Sport participation was associated with favourable psychological and social outcomes; team sport often showed stronger social/mental-health associations than individual sport. The authors note variation in quantitative study quality.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37344901/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10286465/

2. **Andersen et al., Scandinavian Journal of Public Health 2019 — integrative review of team sport.**  
   Team-sport participation was consistently associated with social support, belonging, self-esteem and social interaction, while causal certainty varies by study design.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30113260/

3. **Recreational football literature — exercise-physiology proxy, not Frisbee-specific evidence.**  
   A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis in adults with hypertension found improvements in BP, fitness and several cardiometabolic outcomes from recreational football training. This supports the plausibility of health benefit from intermittent team sport but must not be misrepresented as a trial of Frisbee.  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12577365/

### Conclusion

**Protect Frisbee because Josh enjoys and adheres to it.** The strongest evidence is for physical activity + adult sport/social participation generally, not for a unique medicinal effect of Frisbee itself.

## 3. Saturday parkrun/run-walk as family aerobic work

### Josh-specific reason

Saturday 5 km parkrun is:
- repeatable;
- inexpensive;
- family-compatible;
- usable as a long-term fitness benchmark.

Most weeks it is prescribed as conversational/easy, not a race.

### Official guidance

It contributes to regular moderate-to-vigorous physical activity:
- https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years

### Evidence interpretation

The health claim is **not** that “parkrun itself is medically superior”. The evidence-backed behaviour is regular aerobic activity.

The 5 km format is a **measurement/adherence choice** because it is free, standardised and already part of Josh's life.

## 4. Maintain roughly 7–9 hours of good-quality sleep with regular timing

### Josh-specific reason

Older 2026 data contained short-sleep periods, but recent weeks are much better—around 8 hours in Google Health and ~7.7 hours stage-derived asleep in recent Fitbit data.

The current goal is **preserve the improvement**, not extend sleep indefinitely.

### Official guidance

Australia's 2026 adult guideline recommends **7–9 hours of good-quality sleep with consistent bed and wake times**.

- https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years

### Peer-reviewed evidence

1. **2025 Sleep Medicine Reviews systematic review of sleep regularity.**  
   Synthesises adult evidence linking irregular sleep timing with multiple mental, cardiometabolic, inflammatory and mortality-related outcomes, while noting the limitations of heterogeneous observational studies.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41259946/  
   DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2025.102203

2. **Wang et al. 2022 meta-review/meta-analysis.**  
   Synthesised observational and Mendelian-randomisation evidence connecting short sleep with cardiovascular outcomes; causal interpretation differs by outcome.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36035915/

3. **García-Perdomo et al. 2019 systematic review/meta-analysis.**  
   Used 7–9 h as the reference category when studying sleep duration and mortality; long sleep may also reflect underlying illness, so “more sleep” is not automatically better.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30058510/

### Conclusion

Josh's current recent sleep amount is **not an obvious deficit**. Maintain regularity and adequate sleep; do not chase 9–10+ hours merely to improve a wearable score.

## 5. Vegetable target: 6 Australian serves/day ≈ 450 g

### Correction

The old Josh Plan used **375 g / 5 serves**. That was too generic.

For Australian men aged **19–50**, Eat For Health recommends **6 serves of vegetables/legumes per day**. One vegetable serve is approximately **75 g**, making the simple weight equivalent about **450 g/day**.

Official:
- https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/how-much-do-we-need-each-day/recommended-number-serves-adults
- https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/food-essentials/five-food-groups/vegetables-and-legumes-beans

The same table recommends **2 fruit serves/day**.

### Evidence context

This is an Australian dietary-pattern recommendation, not a claim that exactly 450 g is a biological threshold.

## 6. Fibre target: 30 g/day

### Official guidance

Australian/New Zealand Nutrient Reference Values set the Adequate Intake for dietary fibre at **30 g/day for adult men**.

- https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/nutrient-reference-values/nutrients/dietary-fibre

### Peer-reviewed evidence

1. **Threapleton et al., BMJ 2013 — systematic review/dose-response meta-analysis.**  
   Total dietary fibre was inversely associated with cardiovascular and coronary-heart-disease risk.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355537/  
   https://www.bmj.com/content/347/bmj.f6879

2. **2023 systematic review/dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohorts.**  
   Higher dietary fibre intake was associated with lower all-cause, cardiovascular and cancer mortality, with the usual limitations of observational nutrition evidence.  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579821/

3. **Reynolds et al.-style cardiovascular fibre evidence in CVD/hypertension populations, 2022 review.**  
   Trials/cohorts supported improvements in several cardiometabolic outcomes when fibre intake increased.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35449060/

### Conclusion

30 g/day is a clean official target that also fits the broader evidence base.

## 7. Protein: 85 g/day is a personal target, not an official requirement

At 77.4 kg:
- **85 g/day ≈ 1.10 g/kg/day**

### Official guidance

Australian RDI for men aged **31–50**:
- **64 g/day**
- approximately **0.84 g/kg/day** in the reference framework.

Official:
- https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/nutrient-reference-values/nutrients/protein

### Peer-reviewed evidence

1. **Morton et al., BJSM 2018 — systematic review/meta-regression.**  
   Protein supplementation modestly augmented resistance-training gains in lean mass and strength; additional fat-free-mass benefit plateaued around total intake ~1.6 g/kg/day.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/

2. **Nunes et al. 2022 — systematic review/meta-analysis of healthy adults.**  
   Increasing protein produced small additional lean-mass/strength benefits mainly in conjunction with resistance exercise; the evidence did **not** establish a need for every healthy adult to eat very high protein.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978023/

3. **Tagawa et al. 2022 — dose-response meta-analysis.**  
   Higher protein augmented strength gains when combined with resistance training, with benefit increasing up to higher intakes; protein without resistance training did not meaningfully increase strength.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36057893/  
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9441410/

### Why 85 g?

It is a **low-cost, moderate personal target**:
- above the Australian RDI;
- compatible with Josh's existing food preferences;
- easy enough to sustain;
- not presented as “optimal hypertrophy protein”.

If deliberate muscle gain later becomes a priority, protein intake can be reconsidered alongside total energy and training—not automatically escalated because a biohacker uses more.

## 8. Mediterranean-ish / plant-forward pattern

### Josh-specific reason

Josh prefers:
- dairy-free eating;
- legumes, fish, eggs and plant foods;
- lower saturated fat;
- ordinary groceries.

A Mediterranean-style pattern maps well to those preferences without requiring branded products.

### Official Australian framework

The Australian Dietary Guidelines emphasise:
- vegetables/legumes;
- fruit;
- mostly wholegrain cereals;
- lean meats/fish/eggs/tofu/nuts/seeds/legumes;
- milk/yoghurt/cheese **or alternatives**;
- unsaturated fats in small amounts.

- https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines/australian-dietary-guidelines-1-5

### Peer-reviewed evidence

1. **2024 meta-analysis of long-term randomised trials.**  
   Mediterranean-diet interventions reduced several major cardiovascular outcomes versus control diets, although heterogeneity was substantial and all-cause mortality was not significantly reduced in the pooled analysis.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38431146/

2. **2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of 26 RCTs in overweight/obese adults.**  
   Found modest improvements in BMI, waist circumference, triglycerides and fatty-liver index; evidence quality for many outcomes was low/very low.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39783962/

3. **2026 systematic review/meta-analysis for primary CVD prevention.**  
   Higher Mediterranean-diet adherence was associated with lower cardiovascular risk across a large body of observational and trial evidence.  
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42090744/

### Conclusion

Use “Mediterranean-ish” to describe an **overall food pattern**, not a magical diet or a reason to buy expensive olive oil/supplements.

## 9. Home blood pressure: one properly performed 7-day block is higher value than random readings

### Josh-specific reason

Historical clinic/donation readings span approximately **122–136 / 70–87 mmHg**. There is no clean home average.

### Official guidance

The Heart Foundation recommends:
- a validated upper-arm monitor;
- measurements for **7 days (minimum 5)**;
- same time morning/evening;
- appropriate preparation and seated rest;
- two readings one minute apart.

Official:
- https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/your-heart/blood-pressure-and-your-heart
- https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/getmedia/3ce7d640-12f2-4e03-94f1-96c289877f38/BP-poster-A3.pdf

### Evidence interpretation

Home BP monitoring is a recognised component of hypertension assessment/management, but Josh's recommendation is **measurement quality**, not a diagnosis or treatment recommendation.

Josh's own Health Connect analyses showed:

- device offsets;
- duplicate synced data;
- misleading mixed-source trends.

Therefore:
- automatic data collection can continue;
- decisions should use source-specific longer trends;
- unusual symptoms matter more than one wearable score.

This recommendation is grounded primarily in **Josh's own N-of-1 data-quality findings**, not a population clinical guideline.

# Evidence quality caveat

Even systematic reviews can pool observational evidence, heterogeneous interventions, or low-certainty trials.

The hierarchy is therefore:

1. official guidance for population recommendations;
2. systematic reviews/meta-analyses;
3. individual RCTs/cohorts;
4. Josh's own longitudinal measurements for personal fit;
5. influencer/self-tracking anecdotes.

No profile in this repository outranks appropriate clinical care.

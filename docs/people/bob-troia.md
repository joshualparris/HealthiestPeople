# Bob Troia (Quantified Bob)

## Why he belongs in this project

Bob Troia is best treated as a **longitudinal self-tracking case study**, not as proof that he is one of the world's healthiest people.

His public record is unusually useful because he has published years of N-of-1 experiments and raw-ish longitudinal tracking across body composition, sleep, glucose, diet, wearables and environmental measurements. His strongest lesson is methodological: collect a small number of useful measures over time, test one change at a time where practical, and keep the result if it improves something that matters.

## Evidence quality

Most evidence about Troia's own health is **C/D**:

- **C — first-party published measurement/protocol:** Quantified Bob data posts and charts.
- **D — self-report:** descriptions of symptoms, diet responses and interpretation.
- There is little independent peer-reviewed adjudication of Troia as an individual.

That means this project should learn from his tracking habits without treating every conclusion from an N-of-1 experiment as general medical evidence.

## The most transferable idea: minimum effective quantification

Troia has explicitly argued that people do **not** need to track everything. In a talk titled "Minimum Effective Quantification", he says useful tracking can be reduced to a few measures taking less than ten minutes per week.

Source:
- https://www.quantifiedbob.com/minimum-effective-quantification-my-superhuman-summit-talk/

This is more useful for a working parent than copying his entire gadget stack.

## What ordinary working families can copy

### High-value / low-cost

1. **Track trends, not trivia.** Pick two or three measures that could change a decision.
2. **Use data you already generate.** Phone step count, ordinary scales, training logs and sleep/wake times can be enough.
3. **Look across weeks and months.** A single bad sleep score or glucose reading is usually less useful than a recurring pattern.
4. **Change one major variable at a time when possible.** That makes cause and effect less muddy.
5. **Write down context.** Illness, travel, stress and unusual meals can explain apparent "failures".

### Useful but optional

- an inexpensive activity/sleep tracker
- a home blood-pressure monitor when clinically appropriate
- periodic ordinary bloodwork through a GP
- a spreadsheet or notes app for trends

### Low-transferability / caution

Troia has experimented with many commercial devices, supplements, fasting approaches and biomarker services. Some are interesting but expensive, weakly validated or highly individual.

A useful rule is: **do not buy a sensor until you know what decision its data would change.**

## What not to copy blindly

Troia's site contains older self-experiments involving dietary patterns, supplements and "biohacking" tools that should not be treated as universal recommendations. His own writing often presents them as experiments, which is the right framing.

In particular, a personal response in one individual does not prove a treatment works for others.

## Best lesson for this project

**You do not need more data. You need a small amount of data that changes behaviour.**

For a busy adult, that could mean:

- weekly exercise minutes
- two strength sessions completed or not
- average bedtime/wake time
- resting heart-rate trend
- weight/waist trend if relevant
- basic clinical markers at an appropriate interval

That captures the strongest part of the Quantified Bob philosophy without turning health into a second job.

## Additional verified details

### His own "minimum effective" target is deliberately tiny

Troia's Minimum Effective Quantification talk says that **three tracked things can take less than 10 minutes per week**. That claim is his own framework rather than a clinical guideline, but it captures an important design principle for time-poor people: a measurement system is only useful if it survives ordinary weeks.

Source:
- https://www.quantifiedbob.com/minimum-effective-quantification-my-superhuman-summit-talk/

### Cross-device comparisons need caution

His 10+ year body-composition archive includes multiple measurement methods:

- several Withings BIA scales
- DEXA
- Fit3D / 3D scanning
- other scales and measurement contexts

That makes the **long trend** interesting while also making small differences between devices or eras hard to interpret as real physiological change.

Source:
- https://www.quantifiedbob.com/body-composition-data-visualization/

### A useful anti-biohacking lesson

Troia's archive also contains many experiments that are much less transferable than the tracking method itself — fasting, supplement, device and emerging-technology experiments.

For this project, the rule is:

**Copy the disciplined question-and-trend approach before copying any intervention.**

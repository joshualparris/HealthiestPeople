# Larry Smarr

## Why he belongs in this project

Larry Smarr, PhD, is a computer scientist and long-running quantified-self researcher who built a multi-year time series of **100+ blood biomarkers** alongside stool and gut-microbiome data.

He is not a useful example of "perfect health": he developed Crohn's disease.

He is, however, one of the clearest examples of why longitudinal health data can matter when it identifies a persistent abnormal signal that deserves medical investigation.

## Evidence quality

- **A/B:** UC San Diego has documented his multi-year biomarker/microbiome work and Crohn's-related research.
- **C/D:** some of the detailed personal chronology comes from quantified-self talks and self-report.

## What happened

Smarr began building time-series data from blood and stool testing in the mid-2000s.

UC San Diego reports that he noticed persistent signs of inflammation. By 2011, one stool immune biomarker had risen to a level reported as 125 times the normal upper limit. Colonoscopy and abdominal imaging then supported a diagnosis of colonic Crohn's disease.

## Quantified inflammation timeline

Smarr's public research talks and UC San Diego reporting make the scale of the abnormal signals unusually concrete.

Reported values include:

- C-reactive protein (CRP): peaks around **27× the healthy upper limit**
- stool lactoferrin: roughly **124–125× the healthy upper limit**
- one published talk gives the healthy lactoferrin upper limit as **<7.3 µg/mL**
- at peak inflammation, one presentation reports **CRP ~30**
- by 2011–2012, colonoscopy and MRI/CT imaging showed a diseased sigmoid-colon segment consistent with Crohn's disease
- the disease later produced a sigmoid stricture; Smarr underwent **sigmoid colon resection in November 2016**

These values are not "longevity optimisation" markers. They are disease signals.

The chronology matters because Smarr did not stop at self-tracking. He moved from repeated abnormal biomarkers to specialist gastroenterology, colonoscopy, imaging and ultimately surgery.

## Why this is useful

Smarr's case makes three important points:

1. **How you feel is not the whole picture.** Some disease processes can develop before symptoms clearly signal what is happening.
2. **A trend can matter more than one result.** Persistent abnormality is different from an isolated outlier.
3. **Self-tracking needs clinical handoff.** The decisive step was not more spreadsheets; it was specialist assessment, colonoscopy and imaging.

## What ordinary working families can copy

### High-value / low-cost

- Keep copies of important pathology results.
- Look at repeated trends rather than one-off numbers.
- Follow up persistent abnormalities with a GP.
- Mention family history and recurring symptoms when relevant.
- Use data to ask a clearer clinical question, not to self-diagnose indefinitely.

### Useful but optional

- a simple spreadsheet or health-record app
- home measurements when recommended
- periodic standard blood tests based on age, risk and clinician advice

### Low-transferability

- 100+ repeated biomarkers
- repeated microbiome sequencing
- research-grade visualisation
- extensive testing without a clinical question

## The central caution

More testing creates more opportunities for false positives and incidental findings. Smarr's story should not be read as "test everything".

The transferable rule is narrower:

**If an ordinary, meaningful marker is repeatedly abnormal, do not ignore the trend.**

## Best lesson for this project

Smarr is a reminder that the purpose of health tracking is not to win a dashboard. It is to identify useful signals early enough to act sensibly.

## Additional verified detail

UC San Diego describes Smarr's self-tracking as a **multi-year time series of more than 100 biomarkers plus gut-microbiome genomics**.

In the institutional chronology, by summer 2011 one stool immune biomarker had risen to **125 times the normal healthy upper limit**. That did not itself become the diagnosis: specialist review, colonoscopy and CT abdominal imaging followed, after which colonic Crohn's disease was considered likely.

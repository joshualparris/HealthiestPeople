# Michael Lustgarten, PhD

## Why he is in the comparison

Michael Lustgarten is a physiology researcher and unusually long-running quantified-self practitioner. He has tracked diet daily since 2015, repeatedly measured routine blood biomarkers, and published years of resting-heart-rate, HRV, body-weight, exercise and biological-age calculations.

His case is particularly useful for this project because much of the underlying behaviour is comparatively ordinary: walking, resistance exercise, food logging and standard laboratory tests.

## Background

Public academic biography:

- PhD, Physiology — University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio, 2009
- BS, Biochemistry — Queens College, 2003
- BA, English Textual Studies — Syracuse University, 1994

In late 2022 he reported that he was about to turn 50, placing his birth around early 1973. An exact date of birth has not yet been verified for this repository.

## Longitudinal tracking

Lustgarten reports:

- daily nutrition tracking since **2015**
- routine lipid measurements including triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL, HDL and VLDL
- kidney/liver-related markers including BUN, creatinine, uric acid, ALT and AST
- CBC and white-cell differentials
- repeated resting heart rate and HRV measurements
- repeated biological-age calculations derived from standard blood tests

By September 2024 he described his sixth 2024 blood test as **test #54 since 2015**.

### Exercise

In 2019–2020 he reported:

- **15–20 miles (24–32 km) of walking per week**
- **3–4 structured workouts per week**
- about **1 hour per session**

- increasingly short rests / higher reps to keep exercise heart rate elevated

On 26 Dec 2022 he reported:

- age: almost 50
- body weight: **68 kg / 149 lb**
- overhead press: **50 kg / 110 lb**

That is a useful functional-strength datapoint, though it is self-published and not a formal laboratory assessment.

## Body weight

During late 2019 / early 2020 he reported intentionally reducing his weight from about **157 lb to 154 lb (71.2 to 69.9 kg)** while modestly restricting calories.

By Dec 2022 he reported **68 kg**.

Height has not yet been reliably verified, so BMI should not be inferred here.

## Blood / biological-age tracking

Lustgarten has repeatedly fed standard clinical blood markers into published biological-age algorithms.

Examples:

- Across 13 blood tests from 2016–2019 he reported an average aging.ai predicted age around **29.2 years** while chronologically in his mid-40s.
- His averages progressed from ~28 in 2016 to ~30 by June 2019.
- In Sep 2019 he reported a less-favourable predicted age of **33**, which motivated dietary/caloric adjustment.
- In 2024 he reported individual blood-test-derived age estimates up to about **17 years younger** than chronological age.
- In Apr 2025 he reported a PhenoAge-style result approximately **22 years younger** than chronological age.

### Critical caveat

These calculated "biological ages" are **model outputs**, not literal measurements of how old the body is and not proof of added lifespan. Aging biomarker research itself notes that the field lacks full standardisation and clinical consensus.

The more useful part of Lustgarten's work is the underlying repeated laboratory data and his attempt to connect behaviour with biomarker change.

## Diet / self-experimentation

Lustgarten systematically changes food intake and looks for corresponding biomarker changes.

An illustrative 2020 example:

- during an experiment with more meat, eggs and cheese, creatinine was **1.08 mg/dL**
- after reducing those foods, a later measurement was **0.97 mg/dL**
- he reported a 2015–2020 average around **0.94 mg/dL**

This is hypothesis-generating N-of-1 work, not proof that those foods caused the change.

He has also explored calorie restriction, protein/methionine intake, fasting, food composition and body weight against health outcomes and his own measurements.

## What appears genuinely impressive

### 1. Long-term consistency

His most valuable asset is probably not a particular supplement or test. It is having years of comparable measurements and enough behavioural consistency to look for trends.

### 2. Strong aerobic-autonomic profile

Historical RHR values in the high 40s and HRV values around 70–86 ms are consistent with substantial cardiovascular fitness, especially alongside regular walking and training.

### 3. Functional strength while lean

A 50 kg overhead press at ~68 kg body weight near age 50 and 12 pull-ups in his late 40s indicate useful real-world strength, not merely favourable laboratory numbers.

### 4. Iteration instead of ideology

He frequently changes his own diet or training when biomarkers move in an undesirable direction. That is more transferable than rigidly copying his exact diet.

## Low-cost lessons for an ordinary working parent

1. **Walk a lot.** His historical 24–32 km/week of walking can be accumulated through commutes, lunch breaks, family walks and errands.
2. **Strength train 3–4 times a week if feasible — but sessions do not need to be exotic.** Basic weights/bodyweight movements are enough to build capacity.
3. **Track a small number of useful things consistently rather than buying every test.**
4. **Use routine GP bloodwork longitudinally.** Lipids, glucose/HbA1c, CBC, kidney/liver markers and blood pressure already tell you a great deal.
5. **Look for trends over years.** One "bad" sleep score or blood result is less useful than a repeated pattern.
6. **Make one change at a time when possible.** That makes cause/effect less impossible to interpret.
7. **Keep body weight and waist in a healthy range without obsessing over a magic number.**
8. **Preserve both aerobic fitness and strength.**

## What probably does not transfer well

- frequent paid blood testing solely to optimise an age algorithm
- chasing a specific biological-age score
- changing diet aggressively around every small biomarker fluctuation
- expensive epigenetic, metabolomic, microbiome or NAD tests without a clear clinical question
- assuming an association from one person's N-of-1 experiment is causal

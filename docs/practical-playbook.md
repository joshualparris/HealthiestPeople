# Practical playbook: the ordinary working-family version

## Goal

Take the useful principles from Johnson, Lustgarten, Svendsen, Pascoe and Kelly **without copying their money, free time, occupations, equipment or experimental medicine**.

This is a health-maintenance framework for a typical adult with:

- a five-day working week
- children or caring responsibilities
- bills and limited disposable income
- interrupted sleep and stressful seasons
- little desire to turn health into a second job

It is general education, not individual medical advice.

## The striking result

Once the expensive layers are stripped away, the five cases converge on a very ordinary foundation:

1. move frequently
2. maintain aerobic fitness
3. maintain strength
4. sleep consistently
5. eat mostly nutritious minimally processed food
6. do not smoke
7. keep alcohol low
8. know basic cardiovascular/metabolic risk factors
9. track trends rather than chase daily perfection
10. keep doing this for years

That foundation is also much closer to mainstream public-health guidance than to exotic longevity medicine.

# 1. Movement: the biggest cheap lever

WHO guidance for adults recommends:

- **150–300 minutes/week** of moderate aerobic activity, or
- **75–150 minutes/week** of vigorous aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination
- **muscle strengthening on 2+ days/week**

WHO also emphasises that activity done through transport, work, household tasks, sport and leisure counts.

Source:
- WHO, Physical Activity: https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/physical-activity

## What the five cases add

### Svendsen
Shows how powerful aerobic adaptation can be — but also how genetically exceptional elite VO2max can be.

**Lesson:** improve your own aerobic capacity; do not chase his number.

### Lustgarten
Historically accumulated **24–32 km of walking per week** plus 3–4 structured workouts.

**Lesson:** ordinary walking volume can coexist with useful strength and cardiovascular training.

### Johnson
Combines structured aerobic and resistance work.

**Lesson:** do both rather than making health synonymous with either running or lifting.

### Pascoe
Adds mobility and balance to strength and cardio.

**Lesson:** physical capability is multidimensional, especially with age.

### Kelly
Exercise in space is scheduled countermeasure medicine.

**Lesson:** when life is demanding, activity needs a place in the calendar rather than depending on motivation.

## A realistic weekly template

This is one way a busy adult could meet the basic aerobic + strength target:

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---:|
| Monday | Full-body strength at home | 30 min |
| Tuesday | Brisk walk | 30 min |
| Wednesday | Run, bike, swim or fast walk | 30 min |
| Thursday | Brisk walk | 30 min |
| Friday | Rest / normal daily movement | — |
| Saturday | Family walk, bike ride or active outing at moderate intensity | 60 min |
| Sunday | Full-body strength | 30 min |

That gives **150 minutes of planned aerobic activity + two strength sessions**.

The activity does not require a gym. Walking, running, cycling, stairs, bodyweight work, a backpack, resistance bands, dumbbells or kettlebells can cover nearly all of the principle.

### Cost
**$0–low.**

# 2. Strength: preserve capability, not bodybuilding status

The useful signal across Lustgarten, Pascoe, Johnson and astronaut conditioning is preservation of **functional force and muscle**.

Useful cheap movements include:

- squat or sit-to-stand
- hinge / deadlift pattern
- push-up or press
- row / pull-up variation
- split squat / step-up
- loaded carry
- calf raise
- basic trunk work

A home programme can use body weight, a backpack filled with books, resistance bands or a small set of weights.

## The principle

Aim to become **gradually stronger at ordinary movements** while staying pain-free and capable.

Pascoe's published grip measurements and Lustgarten's pull-ups/overhead press are useful because they reflect function rather than an ageing-clock score.

### Cost
**$0–low.**

# 3. Sleep: boring, cheap and difficult to replace

Australian Healthdirect states that most adults need about **7–9 hours of sleep each night**, although individual needs vary.

Source:
- Healthdirect, Sleep: https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/sleep

Johnson's most transferable sleep practice is not his sensors: it is **consistency**.

The Sleep Health Foundation likewise recommends regular bed/wake timing, daytime light exposure and a wind-down period.

Source:
- Sleep Health Foundation, Sleep Hygiene: https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/sleep-hygiene-good-sleep-habits

## Parent reality

Children, illness, shift work and stressful periods can make 7–9 uninterrupted hours impossible.

The useful response is **not** guilt or increasingly expensive trackers.

Priorities:

1. protect enough time in bed when possible
2. keep wake/bed times reasonably repeatable
3. get daylight and movement during the day
4. reduce avoidable late-night screen/work spill-over
5. avoid routinely trading sleep for optional optimisation activities
6. seek medical help for persistent insomnia, loud snoring/breathing pauses or disabling daytime sleepiness

### Cost
**$0.**

# 4. Food: copy the pattern, not the influencer menu

The Australian Dietary Guidelines recommend a variety of:

- vegetables and legumes/beans
- fruit
- mostly wholegrain/high-fibre grain foods
- lean meats/poultry, fish, eggs, tofu, nuts, seeds and legumes
- dairy or alternatives where appropriate

They recommend limiting foods high in saturated fat, added salt and added sugars.

Source:
- Australian Dietary Guidelines: https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines/australian-dietary-guidelines-1-5

## What is transferable from the five?

### Johnson
Plant-rich, minimally processed food pattern.

### Lustgarten
Actually records food and changes it when longer-term biomarkers move.

### Pascoe
Fruit, vegetables, nuts/seeds, seafood and minimally processed foods feature heavily.

### Kelly
Even an astronaut's nutritional status can improve through better ordinary food choices.

## Cheap foods that fit the pattern

- oats
- brown/wholegrain bread, rice or pasta
- dried/canned beans, chickpeas and lentils
- frozen vegetables
- seasonal fruit
- eggs
- tinned sardines/salmon/mackerel if enjoyed
- peanut butter and lower-cost nuts/seeds
- tofu
- potatoes/sweet potatoes
- olive/canola oil in sensible amounts

Frozen/canned foods can be nutritionally useful and reduce waste.

### Cost
**Normal grocery budget; no longevity-branded food required.**

# 5. Know the boring numbers

For most people, a handful of conventional measurements are more actionable than 500 exotic biomarkers.

Useful things to know with normal primary care include:

- blood pressure
- cholesterol/lipids
- blood glucose / HbA1c when clinically appropriate
- body weight and/or waist trend
- smoking status
- family history
- age-appropriate screening

The Australian Heart Foundation notes that high blood pressure and cholesterol can be silent. It recommends adults aged 18+ have blood pressure checked at least every two years, and describes Medicare Heart Health Checks for eligible adults from age 45 (earlier for some groups/risk circumstances).

Sources:
- Heart Foundation, Blood pressure: https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/your-heart/blood-pressure-and-your-heart
- Heart Foundation, Heart Health Checks: https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/your-heart/heart-health-checks

## What not to do

Do not assume that because Johnson has frequent imaging, or Pascoe repeats ageing tests, the average asymptomatic adult should do the same.

Screening has downsides as well as benefits: false positives, incidental findings, radiation for some modalities, cost and downstream procedures.

Use a GP and evidence-based screening recommendations rather than a shopping list of scans.

### Cost
**Usually low relative to advanced longevity testing; depends on Medicare/provider and clinical indication.**

# 6. Tobacco: this one is unusually clear

The Australian Government states that there is **no safe level of smoking** and even occasional smoking is harmful.

Source:
- Australian Government, Smoking: https://www.health.gov.au/give-up-for-good/smoking

No wearable, supplement, sauna or VO2max score meaningfully turns smoking into a health-neutral behaviour.

### Cost
Not smoking generally **saves money**.

---

# 7. Alcohol: less beats optimising around it

Current Australian guidance says healthy adults who drink should have **no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than 4 on any one day**; less alcohol means lower risk.

Source:
- Australian Government, alcohol guidance: https://www.health.gov.au/topics/alcohol/about-alcohol/how-much-alcohol-is-safe-to-drink

You do not need alcohol for cardiovascular health.

### Cost
Reducing alcohol generally **saves money**.

# 8. Track only what changes decisions

This is one of the strongest lessons from Lustgarten.

## Free / cheap tracking

Potentially useful:

- exercise sessions
- weekly body weight if relevant
- waist periodically
- home blood pressure if your clinician recommends it
- approximate bedtime/wake time
- walking/running/cycling performance
- strength progress
- how you actually feel and function

## Optional consumer tracking

Can be useful if it changes behaviour:

- inexpensive activity tracker
- resting heart rate
- wearable sleep estimates
- HRV trends
- GPS running/cycling data

Treat consumer devices as trend tools, not diagnostic instruments.

## Usually unnecessary for an ordinary healthy family budget

- repeated epigenetic clocks
- telomere testing
- NAD testing
- routine microbiome sequencing
- continuous glucose monitoring without a clinical reason
- full-body MRI packages
- multi-omics panels
- dozens of supplements chosen from influencer protocols

# 9. Stress and relationships count

A health plan that requires continually neglecting family, friends, rest or meaningful work is poorly designed.

Pascoe explicitly describes letting social opportunities replace a scheduled workout or biohack and returning to the routine later.

Kelly's data are also a reminder that sustained environmental/psychological stress creates measurable physiological changes.

## Practical rule

**Protect consistency, not perfection.**

A difficult week might contain:

- two 20-minute workouts instead of four
- family walking instead of a long solo session
- convenience food instead of an ideal meal
- more sleep instead of a late workout

That is not failure. The relevant unit of measurement is years.

# 10. The $0–$100 priority order

Before buying longevity technology:

### $0
- stop smoking
- regular sleep/wake opportunity
- walking
- running
- bodyweight strength
- stairs
- sunlight/daytime outdoor time
- use active transport where possible
- reduce alcohol
- plan meals
- protect relationships/social connection

### Low cost
- ordinary nutritious groceries
- decent walking/running shoes when needed
- resistance band
- used dumbbells/kettlebell
- basic digital scales if useful

### Medical basics
- GP preventive care
- indicated blood tests
- blood pressure
- vaccinations
- dental care
- age/risk-appropriate screening
- prescribed medication when indicated

### Only after the basics
Consumer wearables or additional testing that has a clear question attached.

### Very low priority for most people
Influencer-grade longevity laboratories, experimental anti-ageing therapies and enormous supplement stacks.

# The family-health version in one sentence

**Sleep as consistently as family life permits, walk a lot, get your heart working hard sometimes, lift something heavy twice a week, eat mostly ordinary nutritious food, don't smoke, keep alcohol low, know your basic medical risk factors, maintain relationships, and repeat for decades.**

That captures far more of the credible signal from these five cases than attempting to reproduce their most expensive interventions.

# Transferability matrix: what can a normal working family actually use?

## The rule

A practice can be associated with an exceptionally healthy person and still be a poor recommendation for a normal household.

We score transferability by asking:

1. **Does it have a plausible evidence-based health benefit?**
2. **How much time does it consume?**
3. **How much money does it consume?**
4. **Can it coexist with full-time work and children?**
5. **Does it require medical supervision?**
6. **Is the benefit likely to be large enough to matter?**

The point is not to reproduce these five lives. It is to identify the **highest-value common denominator**.

## Cost/time key

**Cost**
- Free — negligible incremental spend
- Low — normal recreation/grocery/basic equipment
- Moderate — recurring consumer equipment/testing
- High — specialist/private testing or premium equipment
- Very high — medical-team/advanced procedure territory

**Family compatibility**
- High — easily integrated into family/work life
- Medium — requires protected time or planning
- Low — difficult to sustain alongside ordinary work/caring responsibilities

### The 80/20 extraction

Keep:
- bedtime consistency
- enough sleep opportunity
- regular movement
- aerobic + strength training
- whole foods
- tracking a few meaningful trends

Leave behind:
- the medical-team schedule
- the equipment-heavy morning
- extreme meal timing
- copying prescriptions/supplements

### The 80/20 extraction

Lustgarten may have the best **method** for a non-billionaire:

1. track a few things consistently;
2. walk a lot;
3. train strength/cardio;

5. change behaviour when trends are genuinely concerning;
6. do not confuse an algorithmic "biological age" with destiny.

A working parent could achieve the behavioural core without reproducing his testing frequency.

### The 80/20 extraction

Do **not** copy 10–15 hours of endurance training.

Copy:
- most cardio at sustainable intensity
- a smaller amount hard
- gradual volume progression
- consistency over years
- compare fitness with your own baseline

For a busy adult, WHO-level weekly activity can deliver large health benefit without elite-athlete volume.

# A more realistic time budget

A working parent does not need 6–15 hours/week of formal training.

A practical baseline:

- **2 x 30 min strength = 60 min/week**
- **3 x 30 min brisk/cardio = 90 min/week**
- **1 x 60 min active family outing = 60 min/week**

Total planned time: **210 minutes/week (3.5 hours)**.

Of that, aerobic activity can meet/exceed the minimum public-health target if the family outing and cardio sessions are genuinely moderate intensity.

Even that can be broken into smaller periods and integrated with:
- walking to shops/school/work
- lunch-break walking
- playing with children
- bike commuting
- stairs
- yard/household work that genuinely raises activity level

The aim is a life that is **habitually active**, not a perfect training spreadsheet.

# What to spend money on first

For a finite household health budget, this project's current evidence supports prioritising:

1. normal nutritious food
2. primary/dental healthcare and prescribed treatment
3. suitable footwear/equipment needed to move comfortably
4. basic home resistance equipment only if it improves adherence
5. preventive screening appropriate to age/risk
6. optional simple tracking if it changes behaviour

Only after those are covered should premium longevity gadgets compete for money.

# What this comparison cannot prove

These five are selected because they are unusually measured and/or physiologically interesting.

They do **not** prove that:
- their exact routines caused every favourable biomarker;
- their expensive interventions add lifespan;
- one person's ideal diet/exercise dose fits everyone;
- lower/higher is always better for every marker;
- a person with fewer public measurements is less healthy.

The ordinary-family goal is **high-probability health benefit per dollar and per hour**, not maximum measurement density.

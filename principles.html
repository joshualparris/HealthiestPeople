What each health principle actually means

Clicking a principle should lead somewhere useful. This page explains the practical meaning, the minimum useful dose, common misunderstandings, and where the recommendation comes from.

1

Foundation

Move most days

This does not mean “do a workout every day”. It means keeping your body moving across ordinary life — walking, cycling, playing with the kids, chores, active transport and deliberate exercise all contribute.

Australian baseline30+ minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity on most days

Plus several hours of light activity across the day and less prolonged sitting.

Easy testCan you talk in sentences?

That is roughly moderate/conversational effort for many people. Harder activity makes talking much more difficult.

Busy-day version10 minutes still counts

A short walk is better than turning an imperfect day into a zero-movement day.

What counts?

Walking the school grounds, cycling somewhere instead of driving, a family park trip, mowing, carrying groceries, parkrun, Ultimate Frisbee and a deliberate cardio session can all be part of the same movement picture. The goal is not to make every movement “exercise”.

Why it matters

Regular physical activity supports cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal and mental health. Australian guidance also specifically recommends limiting sedentary time and breaking up long periods of sitting.

What not to do

Do not add an arbitrary step target just because more looks impressive. If your everyday movement is already high, the next useful improvement may be strength, sleep or recovery rather than another 5,000 steps.

2

Preserve useful capacity

Strength twice a week

Strength training is not mainly about looking muscular. It is about keeping enough muscle and strength to lift, carry, get up from the floor, climb, play, work and remain capable as you age.

Australian baseline2 or more days each week

The current adult guidelines explicitly include muscle-strengthening activity.

Simple movement menuSquat · hinge · push · pull · carry

You do not need dozens of exercises to cover the major patterns.

EffortFinish with clean reps left

For ordinary health training, you do not need to take every set to failure.

What does “twice a week” look like?

Two short full-body sessions are enough to make strength a real part of the week. Your current plan uses brief Tuesday and Thursday sessions so strength complements Frisbee, parkrun, work and family life rather than competing with them.

How do you know it is working?

Use repeatable movements and occasionally check whether the same load feels easier, you can do more clean repetitions, or everyday tasks feel more capable. That is more useful than chasing a maximal lift.

Common mistake

People often treat cardio as “health” and strength as optional. They solve different problems. Strength is particularly useful for muscle, bone, function and maintaining capacity over decades.

3

Heart, lungs and endurance

Build aerobic fitness

Aerobic fitness is your ability to sustain activity using oxygen. You can build it with walking, cycling, running, swimming, sport or anything else that raises your breathing and heart rate for long enough.

Most sessionsComfortable, sustainable effort

You should not need every cardio session to feel like a race.

Some harder workUseful, not mandatory every day

Sport such as Ultimate Frisbee already supplies repeated higher-intensity efforts.

BenchmarkCompare you with you

A repeatable 5 km can show progress without chasing an elite VO₂max.

Why not chase Oskar Svendsen's number?

Elite physiology is fascinating but not a sensible personal target. The practical lesson from Svendsen is that aerobic capacity responds strongly to training — and falls when training stops — not that ordinary people should pursue a world-class VO₂max.

4

Recovery foundation

Protect sleep

“Protect sleep” means giving yourself enough opportunity to sleep, keeping timing reasonably consistent, and removing obvious barriers before spending energy on exotic sleep optimisation.

Australian guideline7–9 hours

Adults are advised to get 7–9 hours of good-quality sleep with consistent bed and wake times.

Sleep opportunityTime in bed matters

You cannot reliably obtain eight hours of sleep from a six-hour window.

WearablesTrend, not verdict

A device-estimated sleep stage or score is useful context, not a clinical diagnosis.

What should come first?

Enough time, a repeatable routine, a dark and comfortable room, and winding down in a way that actually fits family life. If your recent sleep duration is already strong, the goal becomes protecting that pattern rather than constantly trying to improve a score.

What does not help?

Reacting to one poor wearable night, buying more sensors, or sacrificing evening peace to “optimise” sleep can make the whole system worse.

5

Food before products

Eat mostly ordinary food

The practical nutrition lesson across the project is not a branded longevity diet. It is a repeatable food pattern with vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, legumes, nuts/seeds and enough suitable protein.

Default questionCan this be a normal meal?

Health should not require specialty products at every meal.

FibreBuild it from food

Oats, legumes, wholegrains, fruit, vegetables, nuts and seeds accumulate across the day.

ProteinSpread it across meals

You do not need to eat protein powder all day to get enough.

6

Less data, more meaning

Measure with a question

Before tracking something, ask: “What decision would change if this number moved?” If the honest answer is “none”, the measurement may be interesting without deserving ongoing attention.

Good exampleHome blood pressure

A proper baseline could change whether you discuss blood pressure with your GP.

Weak exampleChecking HRV ten times a day

More readings do not automatically produce a better decision.

Best habitLook at trends

Repeated measurements with consistent methods are usually more useful than isolated numbers.

Four questions before measuring

  1. What am I trying to learn?Have a specific question rather than “more health data”.
  2. Is the measurement good enough?A wearable estimate and a clinical test are not interchangeable.
  3. What will I do differently?Know the action threshold before you see the result.
  4. How often is enough?Measure slowly enough to see signal rather than noise.

Where this idea comes from in the case studies

Bob Troia's “minimum effective quantification” is the clearest explicit version. Michael Snyder shows the value of knowing a longitudinal baseline. Larry Smarr shows why persistent abnormalities matter when they lead to appropriate clinical follow-up.

Know when self-tracking should stop

Follow persistent signals

One unusual number can be noise. A repeated or clinically meaningful abnormality deserves context and, when appropriate, professional follow-up rather than endlessly buying more wellness tests.

The useful sequence

Signal → check context → repeat appropriately → ask what it changes → clinical follow-up when needed.

Larry Smarr is useful here because the important part of his story was not simply measuring inflammation. It was moving from persistent signals into specialist assessment, imaging and diagnosis.

8

Sustainability test

Keep relationships inside the plan

A “healthy” routine that repeatedly makes you unavailable, rigid, stressed or disconnected from the people you love is not functioning as a good whole-life health plan.

What this changes in practice

Family walks count. An imperfect dinner eaten together can be better than a perfect protocol that creates conflict. Some weeks need a shorter workout. Rest, connection and flexibility are not failures of the plan; they are part of making it sustainable.

Julie Gibson Clark's useful contribution

Her public story is particularly helpful because it explicitly places health habits around work and parenting rather than presenting life as a laboratory.
